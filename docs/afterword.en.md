# Afterword · For Everyone Still Making Games

The book is finished. Time to step out of the handbook voice and say a few plain things.

One thing has been impossible to miss this year: AI is unmistakably an intelligence revolution. It lowers the barrier of entry in every industry, and game development is no exception. Projects that once needed a full crew of programmers, artists and designers can now get moving with one person and a few AI tools; even people with no experience at all can go straight in and produce decent results. There is a name for this now: vibe coding — describe what you want, let the AI handle the syntax, and it runs. So more and more developers are pouring in, and the output is uneven.

Is that bad news? For the careless, yes. For those who take the craft seriously, no.

In Jackie Chan Adventures, Uncle has a line: magic must defeat magic. AI is that magic. The tools are there for anyone to pick up, but what people build with them differs at a glance, and the difference is not in the tool, it is in the person holding it. Traditional developers carry things AI cannot supply in a hurry: an understanding of genre, a nose for player emotion, the instinct for game feel, and a respect for the craft earned from enough mistakes. So my judgment is simple: experienced developers who are willing to embrace this era will hold a clear advantage. In this age, experience is not baggage; it is your trump card.

## Looking Ahead: My Bets for the Next Few Years

- The bar keeps dropping, but the gap between "shipped" and "well made" keeps widening. After the flood, players remember the polished ones.
- AI users will split in two: those who treat it as a typewriter, and those who treat it as a team. The second camp assigns it roles in code, art and QA, and their output is not on the same order of magnitude.
- Judgment will be worth more than speed. Once code, art and copy can all be generated, the person who decides what is right sets the price.
- Small teams and solo builders get more shots. A prototype that took ten people three months can now be felt out by two or three people in one month; cheaper mistakes mean more work breaks out.
- One thing stays fixed: games have to be fun. Fun comes from understanding players, and that is what AI learns slowest.

## So, Don't Panic

If you have been making games for years: everything you have saved up is appreciating. Treat AI as a new colleague, teach it your standards for game feel, let it do the chores, and keep the judgments only you can make.

If you are just arriving: the good news is the door is open, the bad news is it is open for everyone. The way to pull ahead is still the old set: learn the fundamentals properly, build small complete projects, run honest postmortems, and repeat. That is what this book has been about from start to finish. For putting AI into the daily workflow, [AI Workflows](ai/README.md) is a practical starting point. Old craft, new tools: double fuel.

## Closing

The book ends here. The road of game development does not. Tools will turn over, engines will change, models will grow smarter; what players call fun has barely changed at all. The people who make things fun are still the ones who take it seriously.

The open-source projects named in this book are maintained by real people, for free. If you use them, a Star is the simplest thank-you.

May the game in your head one day be real, and played.
